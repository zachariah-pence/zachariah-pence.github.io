Lecture 24: Optimization II (Electric Boogaloo)

Goal: Solve optimization problems involving volume, surface area, and distances.

Example 1.

We want to construct a box with a square base and no top. If the volume is 500 cubic inches, then what is the minimum amount of material required?

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Obj: minimize } A_{(x,h)} = x^2 + 4xh \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } x^2 h = 500 ; \; x, h > 0 \end{gathered} \]

Solving for \( h \) we get \( h = \frac{500}{x^2} \). Hence,

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} A_{(x,h)} &=& x^2 + 4xh \\ A(x) &=& x^2 + 4x\left(\frac{500}{x^2}\right) \\ A(x) &=& x^2 + \frac{2000}{x} \\ \frac{dA}{dx} &=& 2x - \frac{2000}{x^2} \overset{\text{set}}{=} 0 \\ 2x &=& \frac{2000}{x^2} \\ x^3 &=& 1000 \\ x &=& 10 \end{array} \]

\( \xrightarrow{x > 0} \; x = 10 \)

Hence there is only one critical number, it suffices to check \( A \) has a relative minimum at \( x = 10 \). Making a sign chart for \( \frac{dA}{dx} \)

we see there is an absolute minimum by the 1st Derivative Test.

We now find the surface area when \( x = 10 \):

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} A(10) &=& 10^2 + \frac{2000}{10} \\ &=& 100 + 200 \\ &=& 300 \end{array} \]

Therefore, it only takes 300 in2 worth of materials to produce such a box.

Example 2 (Ideal Soup Can).

A company is designing a cylindrical soup can to hold \( 250\pi \approx 785.4 \text{cm}^3 \) of liquid. What should the dimensions be to use the least amount of material?

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Obj: Minimize } A_{(r,h)} = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } \pi r^2 h = 250\pi \;;\; r, h > 0 \end{gathered} \]

Solving for \( h \) we get \( h = \frac{250}{r^2} \). Thus,

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} A(r) &=& 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r \left( \frac{250}{r^2} \right) \\ A(r) &=& 2\pi r^2 + \frac{500\pi}{r} \\ A'(r) &=& 4\pi r - \frac{500\pi}{r^2} \overset{\text{set}}{=} 0 \\ 4\pi r &=& \frac{500\pi}{r^2} \\ r^3 &=& 125 \\ r &=& 5 \end{array} \]

\( \xrightarrow{r > 0} \; r = 5 \)

By similar reasoning to problem 1, \( A \) has an absolute minimum at \( r = 5 \). To answer the question, we also need to find the height

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} h &=& \frac{250}{r^2} \\ h|_{r=5} &=& \frac{250}{25} \\ h|_{r=5} &=& 10 \end{array} \]

Thus, the radius needs to be 5cm while the height needs to be 10 cm to minimize the material used.

Example 3 (Ideal Soup Can II).

Due to the manufacturing process, each can has to use \( 294\pi \approx 923.63 \text{cm}^2 \) of aluminum. Find the largest amount of soup these cans could store.

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Obj: Maximize } V_{(r,h)} = \pi r^2 h \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h = 294\pi \;;\; r, h > 0 \end{gathered} \]

We solve for \( h \):

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h &=& 294\pi \\ 2\pi r h &=& 294\pi - 2\pi r^2 \\ h &=& \frac{294\pi - 2\pi r^2}{2\pi r} \\ h &=& \frac{147}{r} - r \end{array} \]

Now,

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} V_{(r,h)} &=& \pi r^2 h \\ V(r) &=& \pi r^2 \left( \frac{147}{r} - r \right) \\ V(r) &=& 147\pi r - \pi r^3 \\ V'(r) &=& 147\pi - 3\pi r^2 \overset{\text{set}}{=} 0 \\ 3\pi r^2 &=& 147\pi \\ r^2 &=& 49 \\ r &=& 7 \end{array} \]

\( \xrightarrow{r > 0} \; r = 7 \)

Since there is only one critical number, it suffices to show there is a relative max at \( r = 7 \).

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} V''(r) &=& -9\pi r \\ V''(7) &=& -63\pi < 0 \end{array} \]

Indeed, there is an absolute maximum at \( r = 7 \) by the Second Derivative Test.

We find the maximum volume

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} V(7) &=& \pi \cdot 7^2 \left( \frac{147}{7} - 7 \right) \\ &=& 686\pi \\ &\approx& 2155.13 \end{array} \]

These cans can holds at most \( 2155.13 \text{cm}^3 \) of soup.

Distances

Recall the formula for computing the distance between the points \( (x, y) \) and \( (x_0, y_0) \):

Since distances are positive, squaring and taking square roots preserve inequality. I.e., if there is a minimum distance:

\[ \begin{array}{c} [\text{minimum}]^2 \le [d]^2 \\ \sqrt{[\text{minimum}]^2} \le \sqrt{[d]^2} \\ \text{minimum} \le d \end{array} \]

In short: Finding the location where the optimal distance occurs is the same as finding the location where the squared distance occurs.

Example 4.

What is the minimum distance between \( y = 7 - 2x \) and the point \( (1,0) \)?

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{Obj: Minimize } S_{(x,y)} \overset{\text{def}}{=} d^2{}_{(x,y)} = (x-1)^2 + y^2 \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } y = 7 - 2x \end{gathered} \]

Rewrite \( S \) as a function of only \( x \):

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} S(x) &=& (x-1)^2 + (7-2x)^2 \\ S(x) &=& x^2 - 2x + 1 + 49 - 28x + 4x^2 \\ S(x) &=& 5x^2 - 30x + 50 \end{array} \]\[ \begin{array}{rcl} S'(x) &=& 10x - 30 \overset{\text{set}}{=} 0 \\ 10x &=& 30 \\ x &=& 3 \end{array} \]

Just for fun, we see the location where the minimum distance occurs is \( (3,1) \). Now,

\[ \begin{array}{rcl} S(3) &=& (3-1)^2 + 1^2 \\ &=& 2^2 + 1^2 \\ &=& 5 \end{array} \]

However, \( S(3) \) is the minimum squared distance. To find the minimum distance, we take the square root:

\[ \left[ \begin{array}{l} \text{Minimum} \\ \text{Distance} \end{array} \right] = \sqrt{S(3)} = \sqrt{5} \]
