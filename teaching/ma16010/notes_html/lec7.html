Lecture 7 - Instantenous Rate of Change

Goal Interpret the role of the derivative in various contexts

In Precalculus, you were asked to find the average rate of change over some interval, say \( [x, x+h] \) for some \(h>0\). In this case, the average rate of change of a function \(f\) over \([x,x+h]\) is given by \(\displaystyle \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\), the slope of the line segment connecting the end points (See Figure 1).

Now, in application problems, the derivative is interpreted as "change at a point." In other words, the instantenous change at a point \(x\) in the limit of the average rate of change of \(f\) on \([x, x+h]\) as \(h \to 0\) (See Figure 2).

Physics

(Instantenous) Velocity:

Recall the definition of velocity. \[ \text{Velocity} = \frac{\text{Change in Position}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{\Delta s(t)}{\Delta t} \]

If \( s(t) \) represents the position of an object at time \( t \), then the (instanenous) velocity \( v(t) \) is given by

\[ v(t) \overset{\text{def}}{=\!=} \frac{ds}{dt} \]

NOTE:

When interpreting what the derivative, it is important to know the units of the variables in play. Hence in this lecture, unless otherwise stated, \(t\) represents time in seconds.

Example 1

The position of a particle (in meters) moving in a straight line can be modeled via

\[ s(t) = 7\cos t + t^2 \]

What's the velocity?

\[ v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left( 7\cos t + t^2 \right) = \left( 2t - 7\sin t \right) \frac{m}{s} \]

NOTE:

The unit of the derivative is given by: \[ \text{Unit of Derivative}= \dfrac{\text{Unit of Dependent Variable}}{\text{Unit of the Independent Variable}}\] \(s\) is measured in meters (\(m\)) and \(t\) is measured in seconds \((s)\); therefore, the unit is meters per second \(m/s\).

Biology

Example 2

A ball is tossed straight into the air. The height from the ground (in feet) can be modeled via

\[ s(t) = 80t - 16t^2 \]

Before starting the example, we note that this is the usual kinematic equation
\(s(t) = [s(0)] + [v(0)]t + \frac{1}{2}g^2\) you may have seen in a physics class. Here, \( g \approx -9.81 \frac{m}{s^2} \approx 32\ ft/s^2 \) is the acceleration due to gravity.

  1. What is the velocity?

    \[ v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left( 80t - 16t^2 \right) = \left( 80 - 32t \right) \frac{ft}{s} \]

  2. When \( t = 3 \), is the ball going up or down ?

    \[ \begin{gathered} v(3) = 80 - 32(3) = 80 - 96 = -16\ \frac{ft}{s} \\[0.5em] v(3) < 0 \implies \text{height decreasing} \implies \text{Ball is going Down} \end{gathered} \]

  3. When will the ball reach its peak?

    \[ \begin{gathered} v(t) = 80 - 32t \overset{\text{set}}{=\!=} 0 \\[0.5em] 80 = 32t \\[0.5em] t = \frac{80}{32} = 2.5 \text{ seconds} \end{gathered} \]

Biology

Population Growth

\( P(t) \) represents the population at time \( t \). Any function \(P(t)\) that is non-negative for any \(t\) would work. But, here is an example population function:

\[ P(t) = P_0 e^{rt} \]

\( r \): Some Constant (controls how quickly the population grows/decays)

\( P_0 \): Initial Population \(P(0)\)

\( \frac{dP}{dt} \) is called the population growth; measures how fast the population grows or shrinks

Example 3

The population of a city since 2000 can be modeled via the equation

\[ P(t) = 100t^2 - 600t + 10000 \]

  1. What is the rate at which the population is changing ?

    \[ P'(t) = \left( 200t - 600 \right) \frac{\text{people}}{\text{year}} \]

  2. What year is the population increasing at a rate of 1000 people/yr.

    \[ \begin{gathered} 200t - 600 \overset{\text{set}}{=\!=} 1000 \\[0.5em] 200t = 1600 \\[0.5em] t = 8 \\[0.5em] \implies \text{In the year 2008.} \end{gathered} \]

Blood Flow

This equation works for any kind of pipe. Though, we are putting it in the context of blood flowing through a vein or artery. The velocity of the blood is modeled via

\[ V(x) = \frac{P}{4\eta l}\left( R^2 - x^2 \right) \]

\( P \) \(\leftarrow\) Difference in pressure between the start and the end

\( \eta \) \(\leftarrow\) Viscocity of the blood

\( l \) \(\leftarrow\) Length of the vein/artery

\( R \) \(\leftarrow\) Radius of the vein/artery

\( x \) \(\leftarrow\) Distance from the center axis. This is our variable; in this lecture, everything else is a constant

\( \frac{dV}{dx} \) is called the velocity gradient.

Example 4

In a small artery, the velocity of the blood is modeled via

\[ V(x) = \left( 1.85 \times 10^{4} \right)\left( 6.4 \times 10^{-5} - x^2 \right) \]

where \( x \) is the distance from the center axis in centimeters.

  1. What is the velocity gradient ?

    \[ \begin{gathered} V'(x) = \left( 1.85 \times 10^{4} \right) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}\left( 6.4 \times 10^{-5} - x^2 \right) \\[0.5em] = -2\left( 1.85 \times 10^{4} \right) x \frac{(\text{cm/s})}{\text{cm}} \end{gathered} \]

  2. Evaluate at \( x = 0.002 \text{ cm} = 2 \times 10^{-3} \text{ cm} \)

    \[ V'(0.002) = -2\left( 1.85 \times 10^{4} \right)\left( 2 \times 10^{-3} \right) = -74 \frac{(\text{cm/s})}{\text{cm}} \]

NOTE:

\( 1 \text{ cm} = 10\,000\,\mu m \)

\[ \frac{-74 \frac{\text{cm}}{\text{s}}}{1 \text{ cm}} = \frac{-74 \cdot 10000 \frac{\mu m}{\text{s}}}{1 \cdot 10000\, \mu m} = -74 \frac{\mu m/\text{s}}{\mu m} \]

I.e., when we move \( 1\, \mu m \) away from the center of the artery, the velocity decreases by \( 74\, \mu m/\text{s} \).

Economics

Cost Functions

The cost function \( P = C(q) \) represents the cost to produce \( q \) items.

\( C(0) \) is called the overhead costs.

\( \frac{dP}{dq} = \frac{dC}{dq} \) is called the marginal cost.

NOTE:

It is common to model cost functions as cubics.

\[ C(q) = a + bq - cq^2 + dq^3 \]

\( a = C(0) = \) Overhead costs

\( b = C'(0) = \) Initial Marginal Cost

\( c > 0 \) and \( d > 0 \) determine the point of diminishing returns.

Example 5

In USD, a company estimates the cost of producing \( q \) items as

\[ C(q) = 10000 + 5q + 0.01 q^3 \]

What is the marginal cost at 50 items?

\[ \begin{gathered} C'(q) = 5 + 0.03 q^2 \\[0.5em] C'(50) = 5 + 0.03(50)^2 = \$80/\text{item} \end{gathered} \]

NOTE:

\[ C'(50) \approx \frac{C(51) - C(50)}{51 - 50} = \$81.51/\text{item} \]

So, if we have already made 50 items, \( C'(50) \) is roughly how much money we need to produce the 51st item.

Profit

The Revenue Function \( R(q) \) is the amount of money made (without considering costs) after producing \( q \) items.

Profit \( \overset{\text{def}}{=} \) Revenue \( - \) Cost

So, the Profit function \( P(q) \) is defined by

\[ P(q) \overset{\text{def}}{=} R(q) - C(q) \]

Example 6

In USD, if the revenue function is \( R(q) = q \) and \( C(q) = 50 - 6q^2 + 4q^3 \), what is the marginal profit.

\[ \begin{array}{rl} P'(q) &= R'(q) - C'(q) \\ &= [q]' - [50 - 6q^2 + 4q^3]' \\ &= 1 - [- 12q + 12q^2] \\ &= \$(-12q^2 + 12q + 1)/\text{item} \end{array} \] If you were to graph \(P\) and \(P'\) you will see that... you're not making any profit.
