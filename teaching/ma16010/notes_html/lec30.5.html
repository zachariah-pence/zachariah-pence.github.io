Comparison of Integrals and the ML-Inequality (NON-EXAMINABLE):

As we will see later in the semester, it can be very difficult to find the exact area on the curve. In applications, it is usually good enough to get an approximate area. One way we can go this is by "sandwiching" the exact value of the integral between two numbers, then taking the average. To do so, we will need the ML-Inequality (for "Maximum-Length").

Theorem (Comparisons). Let \( f \) and \( g \) be continuous on \( [a,b] \).

(i) If \( f(x) \ge g(x) \) on \( [a,b] \), then \( \int_a^b f(x)\, dx \ge \int_a^b g(x)\, dx \). In particular, \( \int_a^b f(x) \ge 0 \) if \( f(x) \ge 0 \).

(ii) \( \left| \int_a^b f(x)\, dx \right| \le \int_a^b |f(x)|\, dx \).

(iii) (ML-Inequality) Let \( M \) and \( m \) be the maximum and minimum of \( f \) on \( [a,b] \), respectively. Then,

\[ m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f(x)\, dx \le M(b-a) \]

Why (iii)?

Example 1. (Example 9 in Lecture 30)

Give a crude estimate of \( \ln 2 = \int_1^2 \frac{1}{t}\, dt \).

The maximum and minimum of \(\frac{1}{t}\) on \([1,2]\) are \(1\) and \(1/2\), respectively. So, by the ML-Inequality:

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{1}{2}(2-1) \le \int_1^2 \frac{1}{t}\, dt = \ln 2 \le 1(2-1) \\[0.5em] \frac{1}{2} \le \ln 2 \le 1 \\[0.5em] \ln 2 \approx \frac{1 + \frac{1}{2}}{2} = 0.75 \end{gathered} \]

I.e., we get an approximate by taking the average of the upper and lower bound. For reference, \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\), so this is a rather crude approximation (\(8.22\%\) error). We will see in Lecture 33 how we can get a better approximation. The main benefit of doing this is it is relatively quick to compute (at least compared to the method in Lecture 33).
