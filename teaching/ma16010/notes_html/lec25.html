Cost Functions

Factoring in costs can be incorporated in a variety of ways. For example, it could act as an objective function:

Example 1

If the cost of fencing material is $55/ft, what is the minimum cost to produce an enclosed space (as shown in Figure 1) with an area of 490 000ft\(^2\).

We calculate the cost of produing such an enclosure:

\[ \text{Cost} = \left[\begin{array}{c}\text{Price} \\\hline \text{ft. of fence}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\text{amount of ft.}\\ \text{used}\end{array}\right] = 55(2w + \ell)\]

Therefore,

\[ \begin{gathered}\text{Objective: Minimize } C(\ell, w) = 55(2w + \ell) \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } \ell w = 490\,000 \end{gathered} \]

Solving for \( \ell \) we get \( \ell = \frac{490000}{w} \). So

\[ \begin{array}{rl} C(\ell, w) &= 55(2w + \ell)\\ C(w) &= 55\left(2w + \frac{490000}{w}\right)\\ C(w) &= 110w + \frac{26\,950\,000}{w}\\ \frac{dC}{dw} &= 110 - \frac{26\,950\,000}{w^2} \quad \underset{=}{\text{set}}~~ 0\\ 110 &= \frac{26\,950\,000}{w^2} \\[0.5em] w^2 &= \frac{26\,950\,000}{110} \\[0.5em] w^2 &= 245\,000\\ w &= \sqrt{245\,000}\\ w &= \sqrt{122\,500 \cdot 2}\\ w &= 350\sqrt{2} \end{array} \]

Since there is only one critical number, to show there is an absolute minimum it suffices to show there is a relative minimum.

From a sign chart

we indeed have a relative minimum by the 1st derivative test.

We now find the minimum cost:

\[ \begin{array}{rl} C(x) &= 110w + \frac{26\,950\,000}{w}\\ C(350\sqrt{2}) &= 77000\sqrt{2} \approx \$108{,}894.44 \end{array} \]

Example 2

Jessica wants to make a box such that:

  1. The volume is 144ft\(^3\);
  2. The length is double the width;
  3. The top and bottom are made out of metal;
  4. The sides are made out of wood.

If the cost of wood is $10/ft\(^2\) and the cost of metal is $20/ft\(^2\), what should the dimensions be to make the box as cheaply as possible?

Our objective function is the cost to make such a box. We find a formula for the cost.

\[ \begin{array}{rl} \text{Cost} &= \left[\begin{array}{c}\text{Cost of}\\ \text{Wood}\end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{c}\text{Cost of}\\ \text{Metal}\end{array}\right]\\[2ex] &= \left[\begin{array}{c}\dfrac{\text{Price of Wood}}{\#\ \text{sq. ft.}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\#\ \text{of}\\ \text{sq-}\\ \text{ft.}\end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{c}\dfrac{\text{Price of metal}}{\#\text{sq. ft.}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\#\ \text{of}\\ \text{sq. ft.}\end{array}\right]\\[2ex] &= 10(2wh + 2(2wh)) + 20(2(2w^2)) \end{array} \] \[ \begin{gathered} \text{Objective: minimize } C_{(w,h)} = 60hw + 80w^2 \\[0.5em] \text{Given: } 144 = 2w^2h \end{gathered} \]

Solving for \( h \) we get \( h = \frac{72}{w^2} \). So,

\[ \begin{array}{rl} C(w,h) &= 60hw + 80w^2\\ C(w) &= 60\left(\frac{72}{w^2}\right)w + 80w^2\\ C(w) &= \frac{4320}{w} + 80w^2\\ \frac{dC}{dw} &= -\frac{4320}{w^2} + 160w \quad \underset{=}{\text{set}}~ 0 \\ 160w &= \frac{4320}{w^2}\\ w^3 &= \frac{4320}{160}\\ w^3 &= 27\\ w &= 3\end{array} \]

where the principal branch is taken since \( w > 0 \).

NOTE:

We won't verify it's a minimum from now on, but take a moment to convince yourself \( C \) has an absolute minimum at \( w = 3 \).

We answer the question by finding the dimensions of the box.

Width : 3

Length : \( 2w \Rightarrow 2(3) = 6 \)

Height : \( h = \frac{72}{w^2} \Rightarrow h = \frac{72}{3^2} = 8 \)

Therefore, the box needs to be \( 3' \times 6' \times 8' \) to minimize costs.

In each of the previous example, we want to minimize costs (i.e., it was our objective function). That being said, costs can also act as a constraint:

Example 3.

Using the same setup as Example 2., what is the largest box we can make if we have a budget of $240?

We computed the cost of producing the box in Example 2., namely:

\[ C(w,h) = 60hw + 80w^2 \]

So,

\[ \begin{array}{rl} \text{Objective: maximize } &V_{(w,h)} = 2w^2h\\ \text{Given}: &240 = 60hw + 80w^2 \end{array} \]

Solving for \( h \) we get

\[ \begin{array}{rl} 240 &= 60hw + 80w^2\\ 60hw &= 240 - 80w^2\\ h &= \frac{240 - 80w^2}{60w}\\ h &= \frac{4}{w} - \frac{4}{3}w \end{array} \]

Thus,

\[ \begin{array}{rl} V_{(w,h)} &= 2w^2h\\ V(w) &= 2w^2\left(\frac{4}{w} - \frac{4}{3}w\right)\\ V(w) &= 8w - \frac{8}{3}w^3\\ \frac{dV}{dw} &= 8 - 8w^2 \quad \underset{=}{\text{set}}~ 0 \\ 8w^2 &= 8\\ w^2 &= 1\\ w &= 1 \end{array} \]

where we take the positive root since \( w > 0 \). We answer the question by computing the maximum volume:

\[ \begin{array}{rl} V(w) &= 8w - \frac{8}{3}w^2\\ V(1) &= 8 - \frac{8}{3}\\ V(1) &= \frac{16}{3}\\ V(1) &= 5 + \frac{1}{3} \end{array} \]

Thus, the largest box we can make has a volume of \( \left(5 + \frac{1}{3}\right) \text{ft}^3 \). We can check the dimensions of the box is
\( 1' \times 2' \times \left(2 + \frac{2}{3}\right)' \)

Revenue and Profit

Revenue: The total money earned. In symbols,

\[ \text{Revenue} = \left[\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{Unit}}\right]\left[\text{\# of Units Sold}\right] \]

If we let \(p\) denote the price per unit and \(q\) the number of units sold. Then, we can rewrite revenue as a function of \(p\) and \(q\):

\[ R(p,q) = pq \]

Example 4.

A company's marketing department says that the number of units sold starts at 720, then decreases by 15 for every dollar increase in price.

  1. What should the price be to maximize revenue?

    NOTE:

    Unfortunately, there is no good diagram to represent the situation.

    \[ \begin{array}{rl} \text{Objective}: \text{ maximize } &R(p,q) = pq\\ \text{Given}: &q = 720 - 15p \end{array} \] \[ \begin{array}{rl} R(p,q) &= pq\\ R(p) &= p(720 - 15p)\\ R(p) &= 720p - 15p^2\\ \frac{dR}{dp} &= 720 - 30p \quad \underset{=}{\text{set}}~ 0\\ 30p &= 720\\ p &= 24 \end{array} \]

    Therefore, the price should be $24 per unit to maximize revenue.

Profit: The total money earned after dealing with costs. In symbols,

\[ \begin{array}{rl} \text{Profit} &= \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost}\\ &= \left[\frac{\text{Price}}{\text{Unit}}\right]\left[\text{\# of Units Sold}\right] - \left[\frac{\text{Cost}}{\text{Unit}}\right]\left[\text{\# of Units Sold}\right] \end{array} \]

If \(p\) and \(q\) are as above and \(C\) is the cost to produce a single unit, then we can rewrite the above as follows:

\[ P(p,q) = pq - Cq \]

  1. What should the price be to maximize profit if it costs \( \$12 \) to make a single unit?

    \[ \begin{array}{rl} \text{Objective}: \text{ maximize } &P(p,q) = pq - 12q\\ \text{Given}: &q = 720 - 15p \end{array} \] \[ \begin{array}{rl} P(p,q) &= pq - 12q\\ P(p) &= p(720 - 15p) - 12(720 - 15p)\\ P(p) &= 720p - 15p^2 - 8640 + 180p\\ P(p) &= -15p^2 + 900p - 8640\\ \frac{dP}{dp} &= -30p + 900 \quad \underset{=}{\text{set}}~ 0\\ 30p &= 900\\ p &= 30 \end{array} \]

    Therefore, when factoring in the cost of production, the price rose from \( \$24 \) to \( \$30 \).
